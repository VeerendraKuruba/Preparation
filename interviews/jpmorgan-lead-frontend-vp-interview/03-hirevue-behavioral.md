# Round 3: HireVue — Behavioral Video Assessment

**Duration:** 30–45 minutes  
**Format:** Pre-recorded video responses (AI + human reviewed)  
**Eliminates:** Yes — weak answers or unprofessional presentation can end the process

---

## What to Expect

- 5–7 questions shown on screen; you record your video response
- Usually 30–60 seconds to think, then 2–3 minutes to answer
- Questions align to JP Morgan's **4 Business Principles**
- AI evaluates delivery (clarity, pacing, professionalism); humans review content

---

## JP Morgan's 4 Business Principles (Your Evaluation Framework)

Every question maps to one or more of these:

| Principle | What They're Assessing |
|---|---|
| 1. Exceptional client service | How you deliver value to users/stakeholders |
| 2. Operational excellence | How you improve reliability, efficiency, processes |
| 3. Integrity, fairness, responsibility | Ethics, doing the right thing, compliance mindset |
| 4. Winning team culture | Collaboration, mentorship, inclusion, shared ownership |

---

## STAR Method (Required Format)

**S** — Situation: Set the scene briefly  
**T** — Task: What was your specific responsibility  
**A** — Action: What YOU specifically did (not "we")  
**R** — Result: Quantified outcome + what you learned  

Keep each answer 90–150 seconds. Leave time for the "Result" — that's what interviewers remember.

## Common HireVue Questions + Model Answers

### Q1: Tell me about a time you delivered exceptional results for a client or stakeholder.

*(Maps to: Exceptional client service)*

**Sample Answer:**
> "At [Company], our internal design system was causing a 3-week delay for every new team that onboarded — they had to write their own components from scratch. I identified this as a systemic client service failure. My task was to lead the creation of a shared component library. I audited what 8 product teams were building individually, extracted the 30 most duplicated components, and led a team of 4 engineers to build and document them as a shared library in 10 weeks. Post-launch, new team onboarding dropped from 3 weeks to 3 days, and we reduced duplicated CSS across the codebase by 40%. The real lesson was that improving developer experience IS client service — internal teams are clients too."

---

### Q2: Describe a time you improved an operational process or system.

*(Maps to: Operational excellence)*

**Sample Answer:**
> "Our frontend build pipeline was taking 18 minutes on CI, which was blocking 15 engineers from fast iteration. My task was to reduce this without breaking test coverage. I profiled the pipeline and found 3 root causes: a monolithic test suite with no parallelization, unnecessary rebuilds of unchanged packages, and a bloated Docker image. I implemented parallel test shards, added Turborepo for incremental builds, and slimmed the Docker image. Over 6 weeks, CI time dropped from 18 minutes to 4 minutes — a 78% reduction. Engineers reclaimed roughly 2 hours per day collectively, and PR turnaround improved by 40%. We also added build time as an SLA metric so it wouldn't regress."

---

### Q3: Tell me about a time you made a decision that required ethical courage.

*(Maps to: Integrity, fairness, and responsibility)*

**Sample Answer:**
> "During a product sprint, the team was under pressure to ship an analytics feature that tracked user interactions in more detail than our privacy policy disclosed. The business justification was competitive intelligence. My task was to raise this as an issue even though we were behind schedule. I flagged it to our PM and Legal — not as a blocker but with a proposed path: update the privacy policy and add an opt-in consent banner before the feature went live. The business pushed back citing timeline, but I held firm, citing GDPR exposure risk. We delayed by one week, shipped with proper consent flows, and Legal later confirmed we avoided a compliance risk that could have cost significantly more in fines. Doing the right thing sometimes means accepting a short-term cost."

---

### Q4: Describe a time you built or contributed to a winning team culture.

*(Maps to: Winning team culture)*

**Sample Answer:**
> "When I joined [Company] as a lead engineer, the frontend team had low morale — there was a blame culture after a major outage, and two senior engineers had quit. My task was to rebuild psychological safety and re-establish team cohesion. I introduced blameless post-mortems, a rotating 'tech debt Monday' where we addressed long-standing issues without pressure, and 1:1 mentorship for all 5 junior engineers. Within 3 months, deployment frequency doubled, two engineers got promoted, and we had zero voluntary attrition over the next year. What I learned is that culture is built in the small, repeatable moments — how you respond to mistakes sets the tone for everything else."

---

### Q5: Tell me about a time you had a conflict with a manager or peer. How did you handle it?

**Sample Answer:**
> "My manager and I disagreed on whether to rewrite our routing system in a new framework before a major launch. They wanted to ship as-is; I believed the existing system would cause scalability issues within 6 months post-launch. Rather than escalating or pushing hard in a group setting, I first validated my concern by load testing the current system and documenting the failure scenarios clearly. Then I brought the data to my manager in a 1:1, framed not as 'I'm right' but as 'here's the risk I want to make sure we've accounted for.' They agreed to defer the rewrite but allocated 2 sprint cycles post-launch. Six months later, we executed the refactor in a planned way instead of as a fire-drill. The lesson: disagreement is fine, but it needs to be backed by evidence, and the timing and framing of the conversation matters as much as the argument itself."

---

### Q6: Describe a time you mentored someone and helped them grow.

**Sample Answer:**
> "I mentored a junior engineer who was technically strong but struggled with cross-team communication — they often wrote long, unclear PRs that took too long to review and sometimes got rejected. My task was to help them improve without damaging their confidence. I set up bi-weekly 1:1s focused on one skill at a time — first we worked on PR description quality, then on breaking large PRs into smaller reviewable units, then on how to ask for feedback. I also paired with them on 3 cross-team projects so they could see how I navigated stakeholder conversations. Over 6 months, their PR review cycle dropped from 5 days to 1.5 days, and they became the go-to person on the team for cross-team API integrations. The biggest thing I learned is that effective mentorship is about building systems — not just giving feedback once."

## HireVue Delivery Tips

1. **Look at the camera**, not the screen — simulates eye contact
2. **Speak at a moderate pace** — AI evaluates clarity and pacing
3. **Dress professionally** — solid colors, clean background, good lighting
4. **Start your answer immediately** — don't use filler phrases like "um, that's a great question"
5. **Use the think time** — 30–60 seconds is enough to structure a STAR answer
6. **End with a clear result** — never trail off; end with impact
7. **Practice recording yourself** — play it back to hear filler words or poor pacing
